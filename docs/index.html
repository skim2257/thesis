Abstract

Deep learning promises individualized prognosis in head and neck cancer, but the data are not analysis-ready, the models are not comprehensively evaluated and characterized, and the outputs are not usually aligned with a decision a clinician actually makes. This thesis addresses these gaps, arguing that clinical deep learning is a data-engineering and evaluation problem at least as much as a modelling problem and that treating it that way is what makes a model deployable.

The first contribution is infrastructure. We curated and publicly released RADCURE: 3,346 head and neck cancer patients treated with definitive radiotherapy, the largest head and neck CT collection on The Cancer Imaging Archive. We published Med-ImageTools, an open-source Python package, which ensures high reproducibility, transparency, and significant time savings for researchers handling medical imaging datasets. Med-ImageTools is the processing engine for all of the projects built on the RADCURE dataset.

The second contribution is evaluation. Three studies investigate what imaging models learn and their implications for clinical deployment. A 12-model prognostic challenge on RADCURE showed the winning model used electronic medical record features plus tumour volume, and that engineered radiomics predictions correlated with tumour volume. A negative-control framework (READII-2-ROQC) destroyed image content nine different ways while preserving volume: a published survival signature’s performance was unchanged, an HPV-status signature drew its signal from the tumour periphery, and an imaging foundation model’s features derived from the background rather than the tumour. A segmentation benchmarking framework (SCARF) across 12 architectures and four blinded expert raters showed that geometric performance and clinical acceptability should not be expected to follow each other.

The third contribution is decision-aligned clinical models. A patient-level classifier of extranodal extension on radiotherapy planning CT, requiring no lymph node segmentation, reached AUROC 0.913 on the held-out test set and 0.671 on an external cohort, and stratified distant control at least as well as the expert radiologist whose reads supplied its training labels. Chapter 5 presents preliminary work on Dosarex, a fully automated pipeline that reports per-tooth radiation dose on panoramic radiographs for osteoradionecrosis management.

1.1Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) arises from the mucosal surfaces of the oral cavity, pharynx and larynx, and its global burden is substantial, with over 830,000 new cases and 430,000 deaths per year, making it the seventh most common cancer worldwide Bray 2018. Approximately 30–40% of patients present with stage I or II disease, which is curable with surgery alone or definitive radiotherapy alone, with long-term survival in "approximately 70 to 90% of patients with early-stage disease." More than 60% present with stage III or IV disease, which "carries a high risk of local recurrence (15 to 40%) and distant metastasis, with poor prognosis (5-year overall survival, <50%)" Chow 2020.

The disease's modern epidemiology can be largely characterized by human papillomavirus (HPV): HPV-positive oropharyngeal carcinoma (OPC) is a biologically and prognostically distinct entity with separate classifications in the 8th edition of the AJCC/UICC staging system Lydiatt 2017. Its markedly better prognosis motivated treatment de-intensification: O'Sullivan and colleagues identified HPV-positive T1–3 N0–2c patients as low-risk candidates for de-intensification (3-year distant control 93%), while cautioning that N2c patients treated with radiotherapy alone had reduced distant control (73% vs 92% with chemoradiation) and are less suited for de-intensification strategies that omit chemotherapy O'Sullivan 2013.

Definitive management of locally advanced disease is multimodal, either with radiotherapy (RT) alone or with concurrent systemic therapy, or transoral surgery with risk-adapted adjuvant treatment. Each escalation in treatment intensity carries a toxicity cost, with trimodality therapy being the costliest. The central tension to manage is that the choice among these pathways is made before treatment, on imaging, under uncertainty.

1.2Extranodal extension (ENE)

Tumour extension through the lymph node capsule into surrounding tissue, namely ENE, is among the most consequential adverse features in head and neck cancer. There are three ways clinicians determine ENE: pathologic ENE (pENE), determined on the surgical specimen and treated as the gold standard; imaging-detected ENE (iENE), determined by a radiologist on pre-treatment imaging; and clinical ENE (cENE), determined via physical examination. While the gold standard has the highest sensitivity, it is inherently limited to patients who undergo neck dissection. It cannot be determined for the majority of definitively irradiated patients and is unavailable for every patient at the moment the initial treatment decision is made. Epidemiological studies reveal that 54-71% of HNSCC patient populations never undergo neck resection Schlichting 2019Zheng 2025.

Prognostic significance. Huang and colleagues showed that iENE carried a multivariable hazard ratio of 3.86 for overall survival Huang 2020. Meta-analysis across 18 studies (n=3,603 HPV+ OPC) pooled the effect at HR 2.64 for overall survival and HR 3.83 for distant control (iENE), versus HR 1.89 and 3.23 respectively for pENE, and neither form predicted locoregional control Benchetrit 2021. Understanding ENE as a marker of distant-spread biology is what later justifies distant control as a stratification endpoint.

Interobserver reliability. In a prospective, blinded international study, 34 experts (11 radiologists, 12 surgeons, 11 radiation oncologists) read preoperative CTs of HPV+ OPC against a pENE ground truth: median AUROC was 0.64, with no specialty showing performance beyond chance Sahin 2025.

Staging history. ENE entered the AJCC/UICC 8th edition (2017/2018) for non-viral HNSCC: any nodal metastasis with clinically overt ENE is N3b, where only unambiguous ENE, as determined by physical examination and supported by radiological evidence, should be present to assign a status of ENE-positive. ENE consideration was excluded for HPV-positive OPC in the 8th-edition Lydiatt 2017.

In the four-centre ICON-N cohort study, Huang and colleagues proposed reclassifying iENE-positive N1 disease as N2 disease and iENE-positive N2 disease as N3 disease for HPV-positive OPC, with iENE carrying an adjusted HR of 2.43 and a grade-dependent gradient (grade 1 AHR 1.73; grade 2, 2.14; grade 3, 3.64) Huang 2025. The updated 9th edition staging manual reflects these findings to include any extent of unequivocal iENE, when declared with high radiologic certainty, as a clinical N category modifier for HPV-positive OPC, while in nasopharynx only advanced-iENE modifies N3, and in non-viral HNSCC, unequivocal iENE is now accepted as a criterion for defining cN3 disease Brierley 2026.

1.3Targeting actionable clinical decisions

The decision point is treatment selection in OPC, and the cost of error is trimodality therapy. ECOG-ACRIN E3311, a phase II trial of transoral surgery followed by risk-adapted adjuvant therapy in HPV-positive OPC, which excluded patients with matted or fixed pathologic adenopathy, assigned patients post-operatively by margins, nodal burden, and ENE extent (≤1 mm vs >1 mm). Despite the favourable selection, 31% of patients required escalation to chemoradiotherapy post-surgery, and ">1 mm extranodal extension" was the dominant trigger, driving 77% of arm-D assignments Ferris 2022. A pre-treatment iENE determination with known operating characteristics could route patients with occult ENE away from primary surgery to avoid trimodality therapy, while preserving surgical de-intensification for patients without it.

The population-level scale of that cost is visible in claims data. Among the 626 resected patients in the SEER–Medicare locoregionally advanced HNSCC cohort, 56.3% received radiotherapy with or without systemic therapy after surgery: 33.1% surgery plus radiotherapy, 23.2% surgery plus systemic therapy and radiotherapy, while only 26.8% were treated with surgery alone Zheng 2025. Escalation after primary surgery is currently the norm rather than the exception, making pre-treatment estimate of nodal risk clinically relevant and actionable.

1.4Deep learning for three-dimensional medical imaging

Clinical 3D volumes are scarce, expensive to label, anisotropic, and encode signal in calibrated intensities rather than natural-image statistics. Dominant strategies of adapting natural imaging methods include 3D convolutions trained from scratch, 2.5D slice-wise approaches, and axial-coronal-sagittal (ACS) convolutions that repurpose ImageNet-pretrained 2D kernels for 3D input.

ACS convolutions each pretrained 2D kernel of size K × K is split by output channel into three groups, reshaped into K × K × 1, K × 1 × K and 1 × K × K kernels, and convolved over the axial, coronal and sagittal views of the volume Yang 2021. The three view-based feature maps are concatenated along the channel axis, and no 3D fusion layer is required additionally because the view-based output features will be automatically fused by subsequent convolution layers Yang 2021. Since the kernels are split rather than replicated, the parameter count is exactly that of the 2D model and the converted models could load the 2D weights directly, so any ImageNet-pretrained 2D CNN becomes natively 3D.

The volume confounder. A key theme of our studies is that tumour and nodal volume is a powerful, cheap prognostic signal that dominates imaging models. Welch and colleagues’ re-analysis of the four-feature Aerts radiomic signature refit as "MW2018," achieved an external validation C-index of 0.64. Fitting the same signature using features extracted from images with randomized signal intensities achieved similar performance, with C-index of 0.64 and 0.60 for H&N and lung, respectively. Tumour volume alone reached C-index 0.64, and three of the four signature features correlated strongly with volume. The entirety of the signature was a surrogate for tumour volume, and intensity and texture values did not provide any meaningful signal for prognostication Welch 2019. A subsequent study generalized the diagnosis, with nearly 30% of radiomic features correlated strongly with volume, the first principal component of the full feature set tracked volume at ρ=0.78, and volume-independent features alone could not stratify survival Traverso 2020.

The metric problem. Segmentation quality can be measured in at least a dozen ways, and the choice of measure is not neutral. The Metrics Reloaded consensus, developed by an international consortium through a multi-stage Delphi process, treats validation metrics as proxies for the domain interest and argues that inappropriate metric choice is one reason many artificial intelligence developments in medical imaging do not reach clinical practice Maier-Hein 2024. It identifies three classes of failure: An inappropriate problem category, such as framing a detection task as segmentation so that a missed object is never penalized; Poor metric selection, such as using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) where target structures are consistently small relative to the voxel grid; Poor metric application, such as ignoring hierarchical structure when aggregating across patients or institutions. Reinke 2024Maier-Hein 2024. What current literature does not establish is how model performance metrics track clinical utility. It indicates which metric is defensible for a given problem description but does not indicate whether a radiation oncologist would accept the resulting contour. No published study measures the correspondence between geometric metric rank and clinical acceptability directly, according to our best knowledge.

1.5Prior AI work on ENE

Kann and colleagues trained DualNet, a dual-input 3D CNN merging a dimension-preserving and a size-invariant stream of the segmented nodal volume on 270 surgical patients (653 segmented nodes) with node-by-node pathologic correlation, reaching AUROC 0.91 for ENE on held-out nodes ≥1 cm and external validation on two independent cohorts resulted in AUROC 0.84 and 0.90 Kann 2018Kann 2020. However, here are three structural problems with the node-level paradigm.

First, the training population is non-representative. pENE supervision means learning from surgically treated cohorts that differ systematically from the definitively irradiated majority. Since these node-level models are trained with pENE as ground truth, they learn from a surgically treated population that is non-representative of the broader HNSCC cohort, much of which is treated non-surgically Kim 2026. Kann’s study also states its own limitations that there were no cases of ENE identified pathologically when ROI diameter was <10 mm in this study, and so it is unclear if the models would generalize to sub-centimeter lymph nodes Kann 2018.

Second, node-level models depend on a node contour at inference, via either expensive clinician time or an upstream segmentation model. Ye and colleagues automated that dependency with nnU-Net auto-segmentation feeding the locked DualNet classifier Ye 2026. Dayan and colleagues also trained a nnU-Net lymph node segmentation model upstream of a radiomics classifier Dayan 2026. While automated segmentations provide scale, it can become an unvalidated dependency; SCARF quantifies how often auto-segmentations are acceptable and how poorly geometric metrics predict it.

Third, aggregation to the patient level is not anchored to an iENE ground truth. Ye’s AI-ENE model predicted at least one AI-ENE node in 70.8% of patients, roughly four times the 15–20% iENE prevalence reported in comparable definitive-RT cohorts Kim 2026. As the cohort is non-operative, the study itself acknowledges the number of false positives and negatives in this study is unknown Ye 2026. The AI-ENE node count is prognostic Ye 2026, but a determination that flags 71% of patients cannot map onto TNM-9, which is defined relative to unequivocal iENE with high radiologic certainty. Without an iENE ground truth to anchor the patient-level aggregation, Ye’s AI-ENE outputs cannot be directly compared to a radiologist's determination and are difficult to integrate into the 9th edition TNM staging framework. Dayan and colleagues’ recent work on a patient-level iENE model achieved AUROC 0.81 against the radiologist reference, with AI-iENE C-indices exceeding the radiologists' own reads (0.79 vs 0.68 for distant control). However, the study is limited by its single-centre design without external validation, only including HPV-positive OPC, segmentation-dependent model design, and incorporating no supervised deep learning training with iENE labels Dayan 2026.

The main gap in the literature is that no published model predicts patient-level iENE from the planning CT every RT patient already receives, with an end-to-end supervised deep learning model, without lymph node segmentation, and with external validation.

1.6The reproducibility problem

The Cancer Imaging Archive is the largest public collection of cancer imaging, ranging across a diverse range of modalities such as CT, MRI, PET, ultrasound, mammography, X-ray, and also connects with multimodal data sources such as clinical, pathology and genomic data Clark 2013. Radiotherapy planning CT is the key imaging modality for making clinical decisions in head and neck cancer cases. However, H&N imaging was not readily available publicly, totaling ~1700 samples before the release of RADCURE, much of which was not easily interoperable due to the lack of adherence to FAIR principles Wilkinson 2016. The data-hungry nature of deep learning methods meant that this effectively limited machine learning research to only a few academic centers with access to large internal clinical databases.

DICOM, the clinical imaging standard, was designed in the 1980s–90s for interoperability among devices, not for analysis Mildenberger 2002. 3D scans are stored slice-by-slice, so researchers must traverse the file hierarchy and stitch series together, and vendor implementations usually differ. A common technical gap for data curation specific for medical imaging research is that one patient may have multiple RTStruct and RTDose files with one imaging acquisition, one RTStruct and RTDose with multiple images, or worst of all, multiple RTStruct and RTDose files with multiple images. Preprocessing curated datasets for machine learning research from public archives such as TCIA with over 140 datasets and rigorous quality assurance processes, still requires manually reorganizing directories and matching radiation therapy structures.

The methodological consequence is that preprocessing choices are often arbitrary and can greatly impact model training and performance but are not transparently disclosed in publications. Preprocessing is a scientific choice presented as a chore. The stakes were stated by Haibe-Kains and colleagues in their response to a prominent closed-pipeline AI study: "the absence of sufficiently documented methods and computer code underlying the study effectively undermines its scientific value … [and] for scientific efforts in which a clinical application is envisioned and human lives would be at stake, we argue that the bar of transparency should be set even higher" Haibe-Kains 2020. Similarly, the joint ESTRO/AAPM guideline formally recommends reproducibility checklists for AI development and warns that even released source code may be insufficient if specific (in-house) coding libraries prevent reproduction Hurkmans 2024.

1.7Thesis objectives by chapter

Clinical deep learning is a data-engineering and evaluation problem at least as much as a modelling problem and treating it that way is what makes a model clinically applicable. We define a model as clinically applicable if its development study is transparent and reproducible, the model can be deployed reliably without manual intervention, and able to inform a specific clinical decision.

Chapter 2: Curate and publicly release the largest head and neck cancer imaging dataset and publish open infrastructure that easily prepares medical imaging datasets for machine learning research. Hypothesis: automated, scripted curation can supersede manual curation in fidelity while being transparent, faster, and reproducible by design.

Chapter 3: Investigate what imaging-based prognostic and segmentation models actually learn, using a multi-team challenge, a configurable negative-control framework, and a model clinical acceptability evaluation framework. Hypothesis: a substantial fraction of published imaging "signal" is attributable to tumour volume or context, and geometric performance metrics do not translate to clinical acceptability.

Chapter 4: Build and externally validate a patient-level iENE classifier on planning CT with no node-segmentation dependency, explicit characterization of volume correlation, and model evaluation anchored to a clinical decision. Hypothesis: a patient-level model can exceed volume and clinical baselines and stratify outcomes comparably to a clinician.

Chapter 5: Extend the methodology from prognosis to supportive care by building a fully automated pipeline that reports radiation dose per tooth on the routine panoramic radiographs, without any manual contouring or operator intervention. Hypothesis: per-tooth dose reporting can be produced unattended from the planning DICOM study to help dentists make better informed and more granular decisions to manage tooth extraction and osteoradionecrosis.

2.1Chapter introduction

Nothing downstream in our works is possible without analysis-ready data. Med-ImageTools is the preprocessing engine for RADCURE Welch 2024Kim 2025, for the READII-2-ROQC negative-control study Scott 2026, for SCARF’s nine-institution 3,293-patient corpus Marsilla 2026, for the iENE study Kim 2026, and the Dosarex pipeline. That is the argument for treating infrastructure as a scientific contribution rather than as preamble: when a single curation engine underlies the data release (Chapter 2), all three evaluation studies (Chapter 3), and the clinical models (Chapter 4 + 5), the transparency and correctness of that engine become scientific properties of everything built on it.

2.2.1Introduction

In the realm of healthcare research, there is a growing emphasis on the development of automated pipelines and computational analysis of patient medical data. Specifically, in cancer care, volumetric images are abundant and routinely collected at multiple time points during a patient’s treatment Gillies 2015. Recent studies have demonstrated the application of computational analysis and machine learning techniques to retrospective cancer imaging data. However, the often limited size of the datasets used can result in studies with inconclusive results Morin 2018. For robust analysis, particularly in diseases with complex or heterogeneous manifestations, large datasets are critical to ensure comprehensive coverage of all disease phenotypes.

Each type of cancer introduces distinct challenges in diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment strategy formulation. These challenges are further amplified when applying machine learning techniques when adequate representation of disease phenotypes within the datasets is absent. Head and neck cancer (HNC) is an example of a highly heterogeneous disease Lydiatt 2017 that encompasses 13 different disease sites, each impacted by the inherently complicated anatomy of the head and neck. Additionally, factors such as human papillomavirus (HPV) status can impact primary and secondary endpoints such as overall survival and distant metastasis. These complexities present formidable challenges for computational analyses and machine learning frameworks, which could potentially be mitigated by leveraging larger datasets.

Several research groups have employed extensive HNC datasets to glean new clinical insights. Imaging biomarkers or radiomic signatures have been derived using hand-engineered imaging features from images and contours to forecast patient outcomes, often serving as a foundational comparison for prognostic imaging tasks Aerts 2014Vallières 2017. Despite the large dimensionality of radiomic features that attempt to capture various complexities of the tumour from imaging, there is evidence suggesting that these radiomics features are prone to being confounded by tumour volume Welch 2019. Deep learning models aim to overcome this limitation by further abstracting radiomic features beyond hand-engineered feature definitions. These methods have been used for prognostic tasks involving the prediction of extranodal extension Kann 2018, as well as the automation of clinical tasks such as organs-at-risk segmentation Nikolov 2021Oreiller 2022. The success of these studies, and their resulting contributions to both academic and clinical knowledge, is reliant on large, highly curated datasets.

Open-source data repositories, such as The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) Clark 2013, enhance the transparency and rigor of research, granting access to substantial datasets that may otherwise be inaccessible to individual researchers or institutions. These repositories are instrumental in promoting reproducible science and the continuous evaluation of published research. In this section, we introduce RADCURE Clark 2013Kazmierski 2023, a single-institution dataset comprised of 3,346 HNC computed tomography (CT) images with associated radiotherapy (RT) contours and outcomes data that is available on TCIA. RADCURE, with its significant patient count, not only reflects the diverse presentation of HNC patients treated at our institution but also contains the variability necessary to characterize this heterogenous disease site thoroughly. The curation and dissemination of RADCURE aims to stimulate further research and foster novel scientific inquiries in the realm of medical image analysis.

2.2.2Acquisition and Validation Methods

RADCURE comprises data for 3,346 HNC patients treated at the Princess Margaret Cancer Centre with definitive radiation therapy (RT) or chemoradiation between 2005 and 2017. The collection of this dataset for research purposes was approved by the institutional Research Ethics Board (REB # 17-5871). RADCURE is the largest of its kind to be made openly available on TCIA (Figure 2.1), and contains important imaging, clinical, demographic and treatment information data (Figure 2.2, Supplementary Table 1).

Imaging and Contour Information. For each patient, a single RT planning CT image is provided along with the corresponding normal and non-normal tissue contours. Contours are included for the primary gross-tumour-volume (GTVp), relevant gross lymph nodes, and organs-at-risk (OAR). CTs were acquired on Toshiba, GE, and Philips machines. On average, pixel spacing was 0.96mm, with an in-plane-resolution of 512 by 512. Average slice thickness was 2.20mm and the average number of slices was 183. Contrast was administered during CT simulation as per institutional clinical guidelines. Overall, 61.5% of patients were imaged with an administered contrast agent, and this variable is provided in the associated clinical datasheet to allow dataset users to select studies with/without contrast enhancement as needed for analysis.

The GTVp was delineated according to Princess Margaret Cancer Centre’s Head and Neck Site Group’s contouring guidelines. The majority of contour sets were peer-reviewed at weekly site group RT quality assurance rounds.

Demographic, Clinical and Treatment Information. Clinical data, including demographics, staging, treatment, and outcomes, were prospectively assembled via an in-house prospective database (“Bioclinical Anthology of Outcomes in Head and Neck Cancer”) where outcome data was collected at point-of-care Wong 2010. Demographic data includes age at the time of diagnosis and patient sex. Treatment data includes prescribed radiation dose and status of systemic therapy. Clinical data includes the disease subsite, staging according to the 7th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging manual (includes T, N, M and overall stage), ECOG (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group) performance status (i.e., patient’s level of daily functioning as it relates to daily activity, ability to care for themselves, etc.), feeding tube presence, and p16 status used as a marker indicating human papillomavirus (HPV) status. Supplementary Table 1 includes variable distributions for select features of the dataset.

Outcome Data. Binarized 2-year survival and continuous overall survival in months (duration of survival from diagnosis date to death or censoring) are provided for all patients along with cause of death, and time to local, regional, or distant metastasis (Figure 2.3). All data is up to date as of November 2021.

Data Extraction and Translation. An in-house data mining and processing system MIRA (University Health Network, Toronto, Canada) was used to extract and translate RADCURE imaging and structure set data from our institution’s radiation treatment planning and oncology information systems. The input to the data extraction was a set of patient identifiers, radiation treatment start dates and radiation dose-fractionations provided from our institution’s HNC anthology (Figure 2.4).

The mining platform uses a series of data comparisons to select treatment planning files corresponding to delivered plans in our oncology information system. The origin of the CT simulation and contour data was the external beam radiation treatment planning system (TPS) Pinnacle (Philips Radiation Oncology Systems, Fitchburg, WI). The primary CT dataset used for treatment planning is stored in the DICOM format within a patient directory for each radiation treatment plan in the Pinnacle file store. The treatment planning structure set, treatment beam descriptions and numerous dose files corresponding to plan/beam/control-point dose distributions are also stored in this directory in Pinnacle-specific file formats. The treatment beam file is parsed and used in the data validation, but the file is not extracted or translated into a DICOM RT-Plan due to missing beam parameters (e.g. total beam MU). The dose files were not used in the extraction.

Given a patient identifier for data extraction, MIRA locates the patient directory and validates the treatment planning data contained within through a series of heuristics that ensure 1) the radiation dose prescription of the treatment plan matches the input dose-fractionation, 2) the treatment plan has been delivered and recorded in our oncology information system Mosaiq (Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden) and 3) the first delivered fraction was within three days of the input start date recorded in the HNC anthology. Out of the 4130 patients submitted as inputs to the extraction, approximately 3300 matched a single unique treatment plan. The remaining cases, which were abandoned, either matched multiple treatment plans (e.g., multiple ambiguous copies of a treatment plan with potential differences in contour data), or more commonly, did not match any treatment plan (e.g., due to errors in input data, delays to treatment start that resulted in date discrepancies with the input filter, planned split treatment courses that resulted in radiation dose discrepancies with the input filter, etc.).

Upon validation, the DICOM CT image was extracted from Pinnacle and the contour file was translated to a DICOM RT-STRUCT. All RT-STRUCT contours were further validated by ensuring each contour was located on a CT image slice and fully contained within the image bounding box. Manual verification of a subset of the extracted CT images and RT-STRUCT contours was performed by visual inspection and comparison to CT images and RT-STRUCT objects manually exported from the Pinnacle TPS. All extracted and translated data were transferred to a secure storage location on a computational cluster within our institution.

Data Curation. After RADCURE was placed on our institutional computational cluster, additional validation and filtering was completed using both electronic medical records (EMR) and DICOM data.

We filtered EMR data to only include patients with M0 disease and with more than two years of follow-up if the patient was still alive at last follow-up. We performed minimal pre-processing on the EMR data but did perform text case normalization of disease sites.

We further filtered based on the patient DICOM CT Image and RT-STRUCT. Verification of these files included the ability to successfully load/open the files using SimpleITK Lowekamp 2013. The RT-STRUCT file also needed to contain a non-empty gross-tumour-volume, where GTV, GTVp, and high-risk target volume (HTV) were acceptable naming standards. It was assumed, based on institutional guidelines, that this contour did not contain lymph nodes.

RT-STRUCT files were curated to contain the patient’s primary gross tumour volume and organ-at-risk contours. All region of interest (ROI) names were standardized according to AAPM TG-263 nomenclature guidelines Mayo 2018, with an adaptation for nodal targets. All nodal targets appear in RT-STRUCT files as GTVn_[X], where X is the original nomenclature used for that ROI. At our center, all nodes are labeled based on the cervical neck level Grégoire 2014, and if multiple nodes exist an additional roman numeral was added to enumerate the nodes within the level, e.g. the third node in neck level 2B would have a name GTVn-iii2B.

This curation was largely scripted using functionality found in the python package Med-ImageTools Kim 2025. This automated curation process was chosen over human curation to increase transparency and provenance of the data.

2.2.3Data Format and Usage Notes

Data Format. All imaging and structure set data are provided in standard DICOM CT and RT-STRUCT formats, respectively. All data were de-identified and uploaded to TCIA using their standard pipelines. De-identification is a multi-stage process utilizing layers of CTP (Clinical Trials Protocol) clients. Details of this process can be found in the TCIA documentation Clark 2013. Briefly, an ID-mapper file is provided to the CTP that maps institutional patientIDs to a TCIA ID. Next, a burned-in pixel filter checks specific DICOM tags that may suggest PHI on an image – thereby preventing transmission to TCIA. Finally, all images are submitted to TCIA using Aspera enabled high speed transfers that utilize Fast Adaptive and Secure Protocols (FASP). This process is enabled by a script that removes or blanks standard tags known to contain PHI, offsets DICOM header dates and generates unique identifiers (UID) that include TCIA-side embedded root IDs.

Data Overlap with Previous TCIA Data. It is important to note that although RADCURE is a cohort of 3,346 patients, 606 of these patients were previously released on TCIA as the OPC-Radiomics database. These 606 OPC-Radiomics patients have been consolidated with the newly released 2,740 patients and are contained in a single database named RADCURE. The OPC-Radiomics database has been removed from TCIA. However, an ID mapping sheet between OPC-Radiomics and RADCURE ID names is available on TCIA.

The patients from OPC-Radiomics contain RT-STRUCT files that have undergone standardization of contour names consistent with the new 2,740 patients. Additionally, the endpoints for the OPC-Radiomics patients were updated during the curation of the clinical datasheet for the newly published patients. It is recommended that the new clinical datasheet be used for all future use of the OPC-Radiomics, and that previous work utilizing these 606 patients be updated if necessary.

2.2.4Discussion

The RADCURE dataset, with its comprehensive collection of patient images, offers substantial opportunities for both academic and clinical research. It stands as a preeminent single-institution imaging dataset, boasting roughly 3.5 times the patient imaging data compared to other HNC datasets on TCIA. This rich amalgamation of imaging, clinical, demographic, and treatment data positions RADCURE as a prime resource for a host of image analysis research initiatives.

CT images provide structural information of patient anatomy such as the size, shape and relative location of tumours, lymph nodes and critical organs. Additionally, CT images can be used to quantify tissue density heterogeneity Lubner 2017, identify vascular invasion of tumours Zhao 2022 and characterize extracapsular extension (e.g. of cancerous lymph nodes) Carlton 2017. These anatomical and biological features motivate the potential role of CT combined with machine learning for prognostication for head and neck cancer, which may yet uncover subtle image features that have novel prognostic value. As demonstrated by the previous work done with RADCURE Reiazi 2021Arrowsmith 2020Kim 2023, or with other similar datasets, potential studies could include outcome prediction with appropriate baseline clinical comparators or analysis of the relationships between provided clinical, demographic and treatment information and imaging features. Notably, deep learning techniques require more data than traditional machine learning techniques and are highly susceptible to over-fitting Mateus 2023. As such, by providing a larger dataset of CT images (two to tenfold) than used in previous studies that demonstrate promising results, we believe the RADCURE dataset has the potential to power future work that may yield new and more generalizable models.

Furthermore, RADCURE also contains variability in image acquisition parameters despite being collected at a single institution. This variability will allow researchers to gain a better understanding of the impact of these parameters on imaging features. Other imaging analysis research projects that will benefit from RADCURE include quality assurance projects. The dataset has been highly curated, but there are opportunities for research pertaining to artifact detection, DICOM metadata analysis, and the relation of these artifacts to radiomic features, or other intermediate or end point data.

Although the size of RADCURE provides many opportunities for academic and clinical research communities, it does have limitations. A significant constraint is its reliance on the 7th edition of the AJCC staging manual for clinical staging, rendering the clinical data outdated compared to the 8th edition. This limitation is not unique to RADCURE but is prevalent across open datasets, including those for HNC on TCIA. Consequently, any model developed using RADCURE must be approached with caution if it is to be applied to patient cohorts staged under the new AJCC protocols.

Additionally, RADCURE is missing information required for a complete view of a patient’s disease. For instance, RADCURE contains a limited number of clinical, demographic and treatment features. Compared to other HNC datasets on TCIA it is a comprehensive list, but variables such as histology and feeding tube status are limited and may lead to biased results. We plan to include additional clinical and treatment features as they become available. However, certain demographic variables (e.g., race and income) are not collected at our institution. Chart information is also not available for these patients. This includes information like chemotherapy dose and toxicity measurements, which are required for complete clinical interpretation of the data. Clinical and planning target volumes and treatment planning dose data have also been excluded from this dataset, limiting the ability to study institutional treatment guidelines or prognostic modeling leveraging dosimetric factors.

RADCURE consists exclusively of CT images collected for radiation treatment planning. This is in contrast to other publicly available head and neck datasets that contain multimodal images, such as PET (Figure 2.2). These additional imaging modalities may provide important quantitative information, such as standardized uptake values or tissue relaxation time, which are related to tissue function and could improve our understanding of a patient’s clinical indicators and their disease characteristics beyond the anatomical and density information provided by CT Bogowicz 2017. However, many questions still remain regarding clinical utility Bogowicz 2019, reproducibility Mateus 2023, and the biological meaning that drives prognostic modeling based on clinical images Tomaszewski 2021.

2.3Med-ImageTools: An open-source Python package for robust data processing pipelines and curating medical imaging data

Kim S, Kazmierski M, Qu K, Peoples J, Nakano M, Ramanathan V, Marsilla J, Welch M, Simpson A, Haibe-Kains B. Med-ImageTools: An open-source Python package for robust data processing pipelines and curating medical imaging data. F1000Research. 2025 Feb 7;12:118.

2.3.1Introduction

Radiology is a powerful modality of data for clinical work, giving clinicians the ability to see the inner workings of the human body, that cannot be seen from the outside Brant 2012. They can inspect in 2D or 3D, the anatomy surrounding the disease, enabling key information to make life-altering clinical decisions. While regular images taken on cameras or phones are stored in a variety of accessible formats, medical images are encoded in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard file format Mildenberger 2002.

The DICOM standard was developed in the 1980s due to the increasing need for an interoperable standard for 3D medical images across various manufacturers Mildenberger 2002. A key feature of DICOM is the plethora of metadata fields that store information beyond imaging data, such as patient information, clinical variables, and acquisition parameters. As modern medical practice evolved over the years, the DICOM standard has grown to accommodate more metadata fields and encompass new imaging modalities or therapy information Mildenberger 2002. This type of data format is unsuitable for imaging analysis as the relevant voxel array must be manually accessed through DICOM hierarchy Mildenberger 2002. Since 3D scans are acquired on a slice-by-slice basis, researchers must stitch together data from multiple files to create one 3D image, adding delays caused by disk reads and consolidation processes Mildenberger 2002.

Specialties that rigorously use imaging data are heavily reliant on DICOM, one of which is radiation oncology. Images are used along every step of the clinical workflow: from deriving a precise diagnosis, to designing personalized radiation therapy plans, and delivering each radiation dose with the appropriate alignment and orientation with brief scans. While the defined standard serves as a good guideline, each manufacturer has slightly different implementations. This is especially the case for DICOM-RT (radiotherapy), a subset of modalities for communicating radiotherapy data Mildenberger 2002. The DICOM-RT standard includes additional modalities such as RTStruct for contour data, and RTDose for radiotherapy dose maps and dose-volume histograms (DVH). While the broad adoption of the DICOM standard to accommodate for various use cases has allowed it to become the de facto standard for encoding, storing, and transferring of medical images, its comprehensive nature has made it difficult for researchers to navigate for the purposes of imaging projects.

2.3.2Current workflows

The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA) is one of the largest public repositories of DICOM images available, with over 140 datasets consisting of more than 60,000 patients Clark 2013. The datasets undergo a quality assurance process to ensure the recorded clinical variables are coherent and the DICOM files are not missing any important metadata fields. These stringent processes and infrastructure have allowed TCIA to become one of the most comprehensive repositories for biomedical imaging datasets, inviting researchers from different fields to explore new ideas and methods on high quality datasets.

While the underlying data and its annotations are of clinical quality, processing the dataset for subsequent analysis requires a non-trivial amount of effort: manually reorganizing directories and matching radiation therapy structures (RTStruct), referred to as DICOM-RT contours, and radiation therapy plans (RTDose/RTPlan) to its corresponding images Law 2009. This is partly due to the inherently complex nature of clinical datasets, as data is collected on the basis of need and iterative improvements, not structured scientific inquiries. It is also sometimes due to the lack of familiarity from machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) researchers in handling the DICOM files for their analytical pipelines. Typical AI imaging datasets have pairwise associations of one image to a single ground-truth label Deng 2009. However, one patient in clinical datasets may have multiple RTStruct and RTDose files with one imaging acquisition, one RTStruct and RTDose with multiple images, or worst of all, multiple RTStruct and RTDose files with multiple images Vallières 2017. In any of these cases, the directories are not always intuitively structured to help researchers understand which files correspond with another.

Once researchers have successfully curated the dataset into an organized structure for analysis, in order to process the raw dataset into analysis-ready format, they must choose from a variety of processing parameters, ranging from voxel spacing, RTStruct name parsing, and Hounsfield unit (HU) window levels, based on the design of their analysis. While these implicit decisions for image processing are often arbitrary, they can greatly impact model training and performance, but are not transparently disclosed in publications Sabottke 2020. This leads to the difficulty of reproducibility of medical deep learning research, adding another deterrence to clinical adoption.

Furthermore, there are a limited number of software packages that researchers can use to quickly parse DICOM-RT files into analysis-ready arrays. Chief among those, SlicerRT Pinter 2012, an extension of 3DSlicer Pieper 2004, an open-source DICOM visualization tool, has been widely used by the medical imaging community. Despite its broad adoption, batch data processing with Slicer requires custom scripting in Python, to be executed in the Slicer ecosystem. Rather than simply installing a package within their Python environment, users must install the Slicer application and add any other dependencies, not provided by Slicer, into the application environment. As a result, machine learning projects relying on Python for data preprocessing will have their code fragmented across multiple environments–the Slicer environment for data processing, and another Python environment for data analysis and machine learning. RT-Utils is a lightweight, open-source Python package designed to handle RTStruct files with relative ease and simplicity, allowing users to easily export contours into segmentation masks in arrays. However, the functionalities of RT-Utils are limited to the RTStruct modality. PlatiPy is a recent processing library and analysis toolkit for medical imaging, mainly designed for the context of radiation therapy. It features a comprehensive set of image manipulation functions such as registration and atlas-based segmentation methods, allowing researchers the flexibility to process imaging data into any format they need. However, PlatiPy does not solve the inherent complexity of clinical datasets, and researchers must spend hours reorganizing the data into a structured set of samples and labels. The current landscape of open-source medical imaging tools highlights the need for a native Python package that can parse large DICOM/DICOM-RT datasets to an analysis-ready format for ML/AI development in a consistent, reproducible workflow.

To address the limitations of the current software packages used to process medical images, we developed Med-ImageTools, a new Python package designed to help researchers transform complex medical datasets into analysis-ready format with few lines of code. It is also focused on helping researchers develop transparent and reproducible medical image processing pipelines by addressing most of the boilerplate code required for image transformations and processing parallelization. While Med-ImageTools has many modular functions for image, contour, and dose input/output (IO) built on popular frameworks such as SimpleITK, TorchIO and PyDicom, such functionalities are redundant and available in other open-source packages as well. We have tailored our core features towards broader use cases and development workflows, instead of modality or disease type specific workflows, such as BIDS Gorgolewski 2016. Our main contribution is in the development of AutoPipeline and will mainly discuss its functionalities and implementation.

2.3.3Methods

AutoPipeline is the main feature of Med-ImageTools that allows users to easily process raw DICOM clinical datasets into analysis-ready Nrrd or NIfTI files, which are commonly used file formats for 3D volumetric data. It is interfaced using the command line, so the user only needs to submit a single command into the terminal to execute the three core steps in the AutoPipeline process (Figure 2.5):

  1. Crawl: The crawler opens every DICOM file in the dataset using Pydicom, indexing important metadata, such as unique identifiers, modality information, and references to other modalities. This produces a database of every unique image and DICOM-RT modality.

  2. Connect: In this step, each patient’s indices of unique files of different modalities are connected to form one coherent sample. There are various heuristics the user may choose to connect samples. The default option is through DICOM metadata, as datasets derived from clinical practice are expected to have corresponding metadata that references unique identifiers of the parent image or RTPlan. Alternative heuristics allow users to deal with anomalies with corrupted or missing metadata.

  3. Process: All identified samples are processed according to imaging and transformation parameters defined by the user. The user can configure parameters such as the pixel spacing in mm(s), which specific modalities to process, the number of cores they want to use for multiprocessing, and define nnU-Net specific flags as well. The images are manipulated using SimpleITK without requiring any user intervention.

The AutoPipeline feature can be directly interfaced once Med-ImageTools is installed. To install Med-ImageTools, the recommended method is via the PyPI package repository in a virtual environment. Running the ‘pip install med-imagetools ’ command will install the latest version of Med-ImageTools and its associated dependencies defined by the requirements.txt file. Now, whenever the user is in the virtual environment where Med-ImageTools is installed, they can directly interact with the AutoPipeline feature in the command line. The simplest way to use it is through ‘autopipeline input_directory output_directory’. This will automatically process the dataset located in ‘input_directory’ and process them at ‘output_directory’ using the default parameters. Med-ImageTools will generate files that are not analysis-ready images, such as the autogenerated ‘Dataset Index’ (Figure 2.5), and store them in a folder named “.imgtools” at the path of the ‘input_directory’. This is to ensure a convenient user experience and intuitive folder structure by hiding extraneous components. An extended tutorial of AutoPipeline and all its associated parameters are available on our documentation page. At the present time, there are no minimum system requirements for Med-ImageTools as it will run regardless of number of processor cores or memory (RAM). However, if the researcher can leverage greater number of cores and RAM, it will allow the AutoPipeline to be parallelized and process the data faster.

As the crawl and process steps are computationally intensive, all steps in AutoPipeline are automatically parallelized using the joblib backend to efficiently leverage all available computational resources. While the output of the AutoPipeline processing can result in terabytes of images, the crawl is limited to a few kilobytes of a metadata database, making it an ideal asset to share with other researchers as a detailed descriptor of a medical imaging dataset. We therefore propose to attach the crawled metadata spreadsheet to large TCIA datasets to allow Med-ImageTools users to process large datasets much faster and more efficiently. These databases are expected to save up to 1000 core-hours of crawling per dataset, accumulating over 2000 core-hours of computation saved per user. By standardizing a commonly repeated imaging processing pipeline into a single unified package, we hope to improve the reproducibility and transparency of future medical imaging research.

2.3.4Use Cases

We showcase the value of the AutoPipeline implemented in Med-ImageTools v1.0.0 for processing three medical imaging datasets, namely Pancreatic-CT-CBCT-SEG from TCIA, a private liver metastasis dataset and a pre-release extraction of RADCURE, in order of complexity and sizes. In each use case, we initially describe the process.

Using pre-crawled datasets on the Pancreatic Cone-beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)

40 patients with abdominal CBCT scans and their associated contours of regions of interest and other organs, publicly available on TCIA

The Pancreatic-CT-CBCT-SEG dataset Hong 2022 was processed twice using AutoPipeline: once from scratch, and once using the pre-crawled dataset, available on the tcia-crawls branch of Med-ImageTools. Processing from scratch, the dataset took 10.77 core-hours (10:46), whereas using a pre-crawled database allowed the processing to finish in 9.14 core-hours (9:08) (Figure 2.6a). The pre-crawled database reduced processing time by 1.63 core-hours, representing an 18% increase in total processing speed or 2 minutes 27 seconds per patient. The time saved from pre-crawled databases is not a substantial quantity for datasets with less than 100 patients. However, when scaled up to larger TCIA datasets such as OPC-Radiomics (n=606) Kwan 2019 and NLST (n=26254) NLST 2011, it can save researchers 24.7 core-hours and 1072 core-hours, respectively (Figure 2.6b). The resources saved are reported in core-hours to allow a hardware-agnostic estimation of time and cost savings. While it may vary depending on the research infrastructure utilized, these databases can result in significant savings in billing.

Comparing manual vs automatic processing of the liver metastasis dataset

97 patients with abdominal CT scans and their associated contours of liver and gross tumour volumes (GTV), data access described in data availability section below.

The liver metastasis dataset was processed using the Slicer API to export a CT scan along with segmentations of the liver and GTVs for each patient. In the initial DICOM dataset, each patient had a single RTSTRUCT file along with one or more CT series, one of which was referenced by the RTSTRUCT. The first step of the process was to load the entire DICOM dataset into the Slicer DICOM database via the graphical user interface (GUI), to make the data available inside the Slicer scripting environment. Then, we wrote a script to export an initial set of candidate segmentations for the liver and GTVs for each patient, along with the referenced CT scan. This script leveraged the Slicer API to identify the CT series that was referenced by the patient’s RTSTRUCT and used an ad hoc string filtering function to identify candidate segmentations. Finally, we iteratively refined the set of exported RTSTRUCT contours on a patient-by-patient basis, based on visual checking, and physician feedback. Although this step was time-consuming, there is, at present, no substitute for manual verification to ensure data quality and correctness. On the other hand, the initial database construction and export script using the Slicer API achieved results roughly analogous to the automatic output of AutoPipeline. The export script is approximately 170 lines long and took 7.5 hours to run on the whole dataset–this script is available on our GitHub repository. In contrast, on the same 6-core machine (16GB RAM, Windows 10), AutoPipeline took only 2.3 hours with 1 command line, mainly accelerated by parallelization (Figure 2.6c). The authors involved in validating Med-ImageTools’ effectiveness on this private dataset had no active involvement in the development of the package before its application. This highlights the package’s robustness on unseen data and its potential utility for multi-centre collaborations to ensure consistent processing. One use case might be to enable federated learning platforms to automatically process each node’s datasets without requiring any user intervention.

Comparing manual vs automatic processing of the RADCURE dataset

3,219 patients with head and neck CT scans and their associated contours of organs at risk (OAR) for radiotherapy (pre-release extraction; the public TCIA release contains 3,346 patients).

At the time of this benchmark, the RADCURE extraction comprised 3,219 head and neck cancer patients and their radiotherapy planning data Welch 2024. The data arrived as two separately structured exports, meaning the directories and DICOM metadata were organized differently. The directories from each system were restructured using general heuristics, and any abnormal cases were flagged and manually organized. We used SimpleITK and PyDicom to extract the imaging and contour data from the DICOM files, which underwent a similar iterative process as the liver metastatic dataset. The script is over 1000 lines long and takes 30-40 hours to run on the whole dataset using a single core–this script is available on our GitHub repository. AutoPipeline scales dramatically based on the number of cores available, which enables the entire dataset to be processed in 40 minutes using 32 cores, automatically managing the directories from different systems and extracting the contours (Figure 2.6d). These results demonstrate Med-ImageTools’ design does not bottleneck any multiprocessing backends and brings meaningful acceleration on very large datasets, such as RADCURE.

One caveat of these comparisons is that the iterative process of filtering appropriate contours, which can add weeks to months of cooperation between the researchers and the physicians, was already conducted in the original processing steps. Various data cleaning steps that require human intervention, such as sorting contour names or selecting specific subseries acquisitions, cannot be fully automated for the foreseeable future. The Med-ImageTools team aims to add features to the package that will assist researchers in these steps, such as adding a flag to visualize all unique contours without requiring code, adding subseries detection to the crawler, and publishing a set of regular expressions (regex) that can be used to automatically choose contours from prominent head and neck datasets on TCIA. Also, these comparisons do not take into account the time it takes for researchers to develop the manual processing scripts. Hence, the actual amount of time saved for the researcher may be greater than the reported times.

2.3.5Discussion

Although ML and AI promise to revolutionize the way we leverage medical imaging data for improving care, they require large datasets to train computational models that can be implemented in clinical practice. However, processing large and complex medical imaging datasets remains an open challenge. To address this issue, we developed Med-ImageTools, a new open-source software package to automate data curation and processing while allowing researchers to share their data processing configurations more easily, lowering the barrier for other researchers to reproduce published works.

The AutoPipeline feature will improve the accessibility of raw clinical datasets on public archives, such as TCIA, allowing machine learning researchers to process analysis-ready formats without requiring deep domain knowledge. Another exciting potential of Med-ImageTools lies in building automated workflows using AutoPipeline. For a researcher to build an end-to-end automated pipeline starting from clinical DICOM datasets to outputting an inference-ready deep learning model, they could easily develop a reproducible processing step by configuring only the command line interaction of Med-ImageTools, making debugging and custom configurations simpler since the developer would not have to rely on a static script.

While our package aims to address challenges encountered across a few medical imaging labs, we acknowledge that there may be infinite other issues that may arise in DICOM datasets. This is one of the key reasons why our package is open-source for community involvement and contribution. Also, as stated previously, there are certain onerous tasks that cannot be automated and must undergo human supervision. These aspects of researcher-clinician collaboration are an inevitable part of medical imaging research and are subject to delay.

No single solution can completely solve the reproducibility crisis of medical deep learning research, due to a variety of issues ranging from ambiguous data processing techniques to stochasticity of model training. However, community-centered open-source solutions and increased clinical adherence to data standards, such as contour nomenclature Mayo 2018, can incrementally improve research quality and reproducibility, and make medical deep learning research more accessible for everyone.

3.1Chapter introduction

Before building a clinical model, we pose two questions to frame our understanding of the studies in this chapter.

Is the signal real, or is it size? Tumour volume is prognostic, cheap, and already clinically legible. An imaging model that silently re-derives volume adds cost without information. Sections 3.2 and 3.3 measure how much published imaging "signal" is volume in disguise—first observationally, by correlating challenge submissions' predictions with volume, then causally, by destroying image content while preserving volume and watching whether performance notices.

Does a good metric mean a usable model? Geometric segmentation metrics are the field's currency, but the clinical question is whether an expert would accept the contour. Section 3.4 measures the gap between the two for organs at risk; if geometric accuracy does not guarantee acceptability even for well-defined OARs, a lymph node contour cannot be assumed reliable, which motivates the most consequential design decision in Chapter 4: removing the dependency on lymph node segmentation.

3.2Multi-institutional prognostic modeling in head and neck cancer: evaluating impact and generalizability of deep learning and radiomicsCondensed

Kazmierski M*, Welch M*, Kim S*, McIntosh C, Rey-McIntyre K, Huang SH, Patel T, Tadic T, Milosevic M, Liu FF, Ryczkowski A. Cancer Research Communications;3(6):1140-51.

3.2.1Methods

The RADCURE prognostic modelling challenge (RADCURE-challenge) was organized by the Radiomics for Radiotherapy Research initiative at UHN, open to participants within UHN, with a predefined public protocol on GitHub. The challenge opened 14 Apr 2020 and final submissions were due 25 Jul. In total 12 models were evaluated and the winners were announced 31 Jul 2020.

Cohort: Princess Margaret Cancer Centre patients (n=2,552 from 2005–2017, definitive RT or chemoRT, ≥2 years follow-up or death, M0, and no prior surgery). Split by date of diagnosis to simulate a pseudo-prospective trial: 1,802 train / 750 test with participants blinded to test outcomes.

Endpoints and metrics: The primary endpoint is binary 2-year overall survival. The secondary endpoint is lifetime risk and survival curves in 1-month intervals to 23 months. AUROC was the primary metric, with AP as tiebreaker and C-index is used to compare risk predictions.

Organizer baseline models: baseline-clinical (age, sex, T/N stage, HPV); baseline-volume (primary tumour volume alone); baseline-radiomics (1,316 PyRadiomics v2.2.0 features, mRMR selection, grid-searched L2 logistic regression, 5-fold CV) van Griethuysen 2017Ding 2005.

3.2.2Results

The winner was a deep multi-task logistic regression (MTLR) on EMR features plus tumour volume, which outperformed every other model on AUROC at FDR < 5% except the runner-up. Ablation analyses of the winning model localize its signal: EMR-only drops AUROC to 0.798, replacing the volume input with a 3D convnet image representation drops it to 0.766. One combined model using engineered features (number 3) performed worse than the exact same model using EMR features and volume only (number 2), indicating that the added complexity of radiomic features reduces performance. Risk stratification at a predicted 2-year event probability of 0.5 gave hazard ratios of 8.64 (best combined), 5.96 (best EMR-only) and 4.50 (best radiomics), all p < 10⁻¹⁸.

3.2.3Volume confounding

The top models fall into an optimal region of low (but nonzero) volume correlation and high performance. Note that while models 1 and 2 used tumour volume as one of the input variables, their predictions only moderately correlate with volume (ρ < 0.5), indicating they can exploit additional information present in the EMR features. Higher correlation leads to decreased performance as the predictions are increasingly driven by volume only. Most radiomics-only models fall in the high correlation region (ρ ≥ 0.5), although deep learning predictions correlate at notably lower level than engineered features. Interestingly, the best radiomics submission (number 9) achieves the lowest volume correlation, suggesting that it might be possible to learn volume-independent image-based predictors.

3.2.4External validation

The top three models plus the best deep learning submission were evaluated across three external datasets: HN1 (n=137) Wee 2019, MDACC (n=627) Grossberg 2018, and GPCC HN (n=298), an external private dataset. The three datasets are significantly heterogeneous in disease site, HPV status and outcome prevalence. Performance dropped in two of three datasets but remained better than random (p < 0.0001).

3.2.5Deep-CR-MTLR: a Multi-Modal Approach for Cancer Survival Prediction with Competing Risks

Kim S*, Kazmierski M*, Haibe-Kains B. Proceedings of the AAAI Spring Symposium on Survival Prediction. PMLR 2021;146:223–231.

The RADCURE-challenge evaluated twelve models against a single binary endpoint, two-year overall survival, which pools death from cancer with death from other causes, making clinical decisions hard to derive, since those decisions trade cancer control against treatment-related harm. Deep-CR-MTLR was designed to extend MTLR from a single-event setting to competing risks, by fitting a separate set of interval parameters for each mutually exclusive event and normalizing over both time and event, so that the model predicts a cause-specific cumulative incidence function rather than an all-cause survival curve Yu 2011Kim 2021. The resulting objective is similar to DeepHit, but based on MTLR’s unique discretized time bin function rather than a discrete-time likelihood Lee 2018. The model also carries an image arm that the top challenge submission did not, a 3D convolutional network over a 50 × 50 × 50 voxel patch centered on the primary tumour, pooled to 512 dimensions and concatenated with the clinical vector before a shared MTLR head.

The study leverages the same RADCURE cohort and temporal split used for the RADCURE-challenge (n=2,552, with 1,802 train and validation and 750 test). Deep-CR-MTLR reached two-year AUROC 0.774 and C-index 0.788 for cancer-specific death, and 0.781 and 0.761 for other-cause death. The same network on clinical variables alone reached 0.768 and 0.756, whereas the convolutional network alone reached 0.742 and 0.746 Kim 2021. Multimodal input helped where it was expected to, in lifetime risk of cancer-specific death, and the margin was small. Separating the causes of death did not reveal a new imaging signal that the pooled endpoint did not reveal.

3.2.6Conclusions

ML combined with simple prognostic factors outperformed multiple advanced CT radiomics and deep learning methods. Although deep learning–based imaging models generally outperformed approaches based on handcrafted features, none proved superior to the combined EMR-volume model, even when combined with EMR data. The paper’s conclusions match those of Ger and colleagues, who did not find significant improvement in prognostic performance of handcrafted CT and PET imaging features in HNC compared with volume alone and of Vallières and colleagues, whose best performing model for overall survival also combined EMR features and volume Ger 2019Vallières 2017.

Modifying the MTLR likelihood to become competing-risks aware leaves the ordering intact, and the arm that fails to improve is the imaging arm. The ablation analyses and empirical results of the 12 RADCURE-challenge models and Deep-CR-MTLR reveal that the limit is in the information in the features, not the estimator fitted to them. The next study tests this hypothesis directly, by destroying underlying image content and measuring whether performance changes.

READII-2-ROQC generalizes Welch and colleagues’ experiment into a reproducible framework. 3.3.3Results

The Aerts survival signature does not notice the destruction of the image. Across all three datasets the result is a flat line: in RADCURE the original signature yields 0.67 C-index and all nine negative controls are within ±0.02 of it, with the volume-only reference running through the middle of the whole set. HN1 behaves identically at 0.63–0.65 with wider distributions (n=137) and LUNG1 has narrow distributions at 0.60–0.62. No control is distinguishable from the original, and none from volume alone.

The Choi HPV status signature carries real image signal outside the tumour. The signature demonstrated improved performance over the volume-only model when applied to original images while perturbations applied to the Full and Background regions resulted in substantial performance degradation, whereas perturbations confined to the ROI had a comparatively smaller effect. This pattern suggests that the predictive signal for HPV status does not come from features within the tumour but rather its peripheries and outside.

FMCIB reads the background. For the foundation model, ROI-based negative controls produced negligible differences in correlation structure relative to the original images. However, when perturbations were introduced to the background, the correlations diminished, regardless of whether the ROI was perturbed. These features are insensitive to perturbations within the tumour volume and are instead primarily derived from information at the tumour boundary or from surrounding background regions.

Investigating four representative texture features as archetypes and their correlations with tumour volume. Features with stronger volume dependence exhibited consistent relationships with tumour volume across all negative control configurations, with minimal deviation between original and permuted images, as seen with GLRLM Gray Level Non-Uniformity (Figure 3.8e), one of the features included in the Aerts signature Aerts 2014. There are features with weaker volume correlation, like GLCM Cluster Prominence (Figure 3.8b) that remained largely unchanged under spatial perturbation, suggesting limited biological or structural relevance despite apparent independence from volume. Finally, features sensitive to negative control perturbations while maintaining lower correlation with volume, such as GLDM Dependence Variance (Figure 3.8c) represent candidates with potential biological interpretability.

3.3.4Discussion

Predictive performance alone constitutes insufficient evidence of biological validity. Features that remain invariant under spatial perturbation while correlating with tumour volume may not capture intrinsic tumour heterogeneity, irrespective of their discriminative accuracy. Consequently, published radiomic signatures warrant re-evaluation against volume-only baselines rather than acceptance based on discrimination metrics alone. The demonstrated inadequacy of correlation filtering indicates that volume confounding cannot be resolved through post-hoc feature selection and must instead be interrogated directly.

The differential sensitivity of the Choi HPV status signature to full-image and background perturbations, relative to ROI-restricted perturbations, localizes predictive signal to the tumour boundary rather than the intratumoural region, supporting the deliberate modelling of peritumoural context rather than its exclusion. These findings align with emerging evidence supporting the relevance of tumour-stroma interactions Fan 2023 and peritumoural radiomics Sun 2018Zhang 2025 for biologically meaningful prediction tasks. Future works of READII-2-ROQC can compare its additional region types that are already implemented to investigate the peritumoural region including: contraction, contraction ring, expansion, expansion ring, and bi-directional ring.

3.5Chapter discussion

Let’s reconsider the two questions at the start of the chapter.

Is the signal real, or is it size? Prognostic performance in the challenge was driven by EMR features and volume, with engineered radiomics correlating with volume at ρ=0.79–0.85. A published radiomic survival signature survived the destruction of image content across all nine negative controls, an HPV-status signature drew its signal from the tumour periphery, and a foundation model’s features derived from the background rather than the tumour.

Does a good metric mean a usable model? SCARF demonstrated that good geometric performance does not guarantee clinical acceptability and the experts' own contours fail peer review 22% of the time.

However, volume-independent imaging signal is learnable and must be demonstrated rather than assumed. The RADCURE-challenge model 9 achieved ρ=0.22 with AUROC 0.766, and READII’s perturbation-sensitive, volume-independent feature archetype exists. To increase the chances of clinical adoption, a model must be well characterized through volume correlation analysis, external validation, negative-control or ablation evidence, expert-anchored evaluation, and an operating point tied to a clinical decision.

The next chapter presents a model built with these specifications. Each design decision is traceable to a finding from this chapter: the patient-level target and radiologist supervision, the absence of any node-segmentation dependency (Ch.3.4), the explicit volume-correlation analysis (Ch.3.2–3.3), external validation under case-mix shift (Ch.3.2.4), and characterizing the model’s operating thresholds to a specific clinical decision (Ch.1.3).

4.1Introduction

Extranodal extension (ENE), defined as the growth of metastatic disease in lymph nodes through lymph-nodal capsule into surrounding soft tissue, has been identified as a major negative prognostic factor for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and is associated with the risk of progression and distant metastasis Huang 2020. ENE was incorporated into the 8th edition of the UICC/AJCC staging criteria (TNM) for non-viral HNSCC and is now also included in the 9th edition TNM for HPV associated (HPV+) oropharyngeal carcinoma Huang 2025Brierley 2025Brierley 2026. While pathologic indication of ENE (pENE) is the gold-standard of determining ENE, pENE is not available in non-surgically treated patients, which accounts for the majority of HNSCC, nor is it available before the initial treatment decision has been made Schlichting 2019Zheng 2025.

Imaging ENE (iENE) has emerged as a novel prognostic factor independently associated with oncologic outcomes and a detailed radiologic criteria system has been developed and validated for radiology use Huang 2020Henson 2024. Huang and colleagues demonstrated that staging schemas incorporating imaging-based ENE (iENE) show significant improvements compared to TNM in discriminating high-risk and low-risk patients for overall survival, disease-free survival and distant control Huang 2020. This is especially consequential in oropharyngeal carcinoma, where treatment intensity is driven by the presence of iENE Ferris 2022.

Accurately labeling iENE requires substantial expert radiologist training. Sahin and colleagues have demonstrated that precise iENE determination is a difficult task, with high interobserver variability and low diagnostic accuracy without substantial training Sahin 2025. This motivates an automated approach to make consistent iENE determinations. Deep-learning screening systems have been shown to recapitulate radiologist-level expertise in chest radiography and computer-aided diagnostic settings Tavaziva 2022Yacoub 2022. Prior AI work on ENE has predicted pathologic ENE of individual lymph nodes rather than patient-level iENE Kann 2018Kann 2020Ye 2026. Dayan and colleagues recently reported an automated pipeline that segments involved lymph nodes on treatment-planning CT and classifies the iENE status of the largest node Dayan 2026. Similarly, our model is trained on radiation treatment-planning CT rather than diagnostic CT, allowing it to be deployed directly within the radiotherapy workflow on imaging routinely acquired for every patient.

We present a patient-level deep learning model that predicts radiology-defined iENE from treatment planning CT scans, trained directly on radiologist-determined iENE labels rather than pathologic ENE. The model is designed for operational deployment: it requires no manual lymph node segmentation, operating end-to-end through an automated larynx localization step that defines the input crop, and produces a single patient-level prediction interpretable on the same scale as the radiologist's clinical determination. We train and internally validate the model on the publicly accessible RADCURE cohort Welch 2024 and externally validate on an independent external cohort from Maastro Clinic. The full training and inference pipelines, trained model weights, and reproducible software environment are released as open-source resources to enable downstream validation and adoption.

4.2Methods

Datasets/Experimental Setup. We leverage three public head and neck cancer datasets with RT planning CT images to train all models in this study: “RADCURE”, “HNSCC”, and “HEAD-NECK-RADIOMICS-HN1” Welch 2024Grossberg 2018Wee 2019. The “HNSCC” and “HEAD-NECK-RADIOMICS-HN1” datasets used to train the nnU-Net larynx segmentation model were processed using Med-ImageTools Kim 2025, filtering ROI names found in each sample’s DICOM RTSTRUCT and saving the underlying image and larynx segmentation mask into NIfTI format. Med-ImageTools’ AutoPipeline feature handles all DICOM handling, processing logic, and image operations. RADCURE was excluded from the training set for the larynx segmentation model to remove any training biases of locating the larynx.

The RADCURE dataset is a large dataset of HNSCC patients between 2005-2017 and their images and contours collected clinically during RT planning. A subset of 915 patients with newly diagnosed primary OPC and radiologist-determined iENE labels was included in this study. The dataset was split as close to 80/20 based on diagnosis date to simulate a pseudo-prospective trial, resulting in a training set of 752 patients and a held-out set of 163 patients. An additional dataset from the Maastro clinic at Maastricht, Netherlands is used for external validation of the iENE model. The dataset is composed of 175 OPC patients and their treatment planning CT.

iENE Label Determination. iENE labels in RADCURE were retrospectively determined by a single experienced head and neck radiologist from the radiotherapy planning CT. The radiologist’s determinations were made prior to any formalization of criteria by consensus groups, but followed closely to the ASHNR criteria, considering iENE positive only when there were unequivocal imaging findings. iENE labels in the Maastro cohort were also assessed retrospectively by two neuroradiologists, using the modality with the highest diagnostic quality available at staging (Diagnostic MRI, Diagnostic CT, Planning PET-CT), based on previously established diagnostic criteria Hoebers 2022. Only the CT scan of the planning PET-CT was used to determine iENE.

Image Preprocessing. CT volumes were resampled to 1mm isotropic XY spacing. Intensity values were windowed to [-500, 1000] and normalized to [0, 1]. The predicted larynx mask from the nnU-Net was used to compute a centroid, which defined the center of a 256 × 256 × 128 voxel crop fed to the iENE model. No additional contrast or artifact correction was applied.

Baseline Comparison Models. Three baseline models were trained against the same training/test split as the deep learning model to contextualize performance: (1) a clinical model using T category, N category, HPV/p16 status, age, sex, and smoking history; (2) a volume model using the volume (mm3) and largest diameter of the largest nodal gross tumour volume (GTVn); and (3) a mixed clinical + volume model using features from both models. Each baseline was a logistic regression model with L2 regularization, fit separately within each of the four cross-validation training folds. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded; continuous variables were standardized using training-set means and standard deviations. Predictions on the held-out test set and external Maastro cohort were averaged across the four cross-validation folds, matching the deep learning ensemble.

Patient-level iENE Model Training. The iENE model is built on the ResNet18 architecture He 2016 with ACS convolutions Yang 2021, initialized with ImageNet pretrained weights Deng 2009. The ResNet18 architecture with ACS convolutions was selected to balance feature extraction capabilities with computational efficiency, ensuring the model remains lightweight enough for rapid clinical deployment and inference in resource-constrained hospital environments. The model input is a treatment planning CT centered around the larynx, cropped to voxel dimensions of 256x256x128, with 1 mm isotropic spacing on the XY plane and original slice thickness of the acquisition. The training set was split into 4-folds for cross-validation and to train separate models on each data split, serving as biological replicates. Each model was initially trained for 150 epochs with a learning rate of 10-3 with the Adam optimizer using the binary cross-entropy loss function Kingma 2015. For each fold, we conducted triplicate training runs of the model, serving as technical replicates. The best performing checkpoint was selected based on the sum of the AUROC and AUPRC on the fine-tuning portion of each fold. A subsequent fine-tuning run of 100 epochs was performed for each model replicate, and the best model checkpoint across all technical replicates within each fold was selected based on the same aforementioned metrics. This checkpoint is saved as the final model for each fold, resulting in four trained models total.

iENE Model Inference. All code and model weights used to perform inference of iENE is available on GitHub: https://github.com/skim2257/iENE_train/. Inference of iENE on unseen data occurs in two steps: larynx segmentation and iENE inference. The nnU-Net larynx segmentation model requires the medical images to be in NIfTI format. An optional step of processing raw DICOM images to NIfTI format can be performed via Med-ImageTools, which is executable via pixi, as configured in the ‘iENE_infer’ repository Kim 2025. The larynx segmentation model inference is performed using the nnU-Net’s default settings, and the predicted masks are saved in a user-specified output directory. The predicted larynx is used to define the bounding box used to crop the model input for the iENE model Isensee 2021. We employ test-time augmentation (TTA) to add minor variances to the model input, providing insights about model uncertainties and improving predictive accuracy Ayhan 2018Shanmugam 2021. Six translations, 12 voxels in both directions for each of the three axes, are applied to each image, yielding a total of seven inferences per image. Since inferences are generated with TTA for each of the four models, each sample will have 28 predicted probabilities of iENE. While there are a variety of ways to aggregate multiple predictions into a final probability of iENE, we take the simple average of 28 predictions, as we do not have extensive held-out testing data to calibrate or meta-learn the weighted importances of each “configuration” of predictions.

Decision Thresholds and Statistical Analysis. Where a binary determination was required, a patient was declared model-iENE-positive when this probability exceeded a decision threshold τ. The primary threshold, τ = 0.5, is the conventional decision boundary for a classifier trained with binary cross-entropy. We examined additional thresholds, at τ = 0.25 as sensitivity-favoring, screening operating point and τ = 0.75 as specificity-favoring, staging operating point. For our statistical analyses, the same thresholds were uniformly applied to both cohorts without recalibration. Because predicted probabilities were not recalibrated to each cohort’s iENE prevalence, a given threshold does not correspond to a fixed sensitivity or specificity across cohorts.

Because ENE is more frequent in larger lymph nodes, an image-based model could achieve high discrimination by learning nodal size alone. To characterize the extent to which the model’s output reflects nodal size, we computed Pearson and Spearman correlations between the predicted probability and the volume of the patient’s largest nodal gross tumour volume (GTVn) on the RADCURE held-out set. GTVn volumes were derived from the clinical treatment-planning contours, which were used only for this analysis and for the volume baseline and are not required by the model.

4.3Results

We developed a patient-level deep learning model that predicts radiologist-determined iENE directly from treatment-planning CT, requiring no manual lymph node segmentation. The model was trained and internally validated pseudo-prospectively on the RADCURE cohort by splitting based on diagnosis date and externally validated on the independent Maastro cohort. Cohort characteristics are summarized in Table 4.1 Welch 2024.

The final iENE model is a ResNet18 architecture with ACS convolutions, trained across 4 folds of the 752-patient training dataset He 2016Yang 2021. To account for technical variability, we trained 3 technical replicates on each fold for 150 epochs and selected the best checkpoint by the sum of AUROC and AUPRC on the fine-tuning portion of each fold. The selected checkpoints were finetuned for an additional 100 epochs. The best fine-tuned checkpoint per fold was chosen to compose the final ensemble and calculate the following results.

The deep learning iENE model outperforms all three baseline models trained on clinical, volume, and mixed features, across both performance metrics (Figure 4.1, Table 4.2). When comparing AUROC scores, we can see that any model capturing volume information performs significantly better than the clinical-only model, out of which the deep learning model performs best. For this problem involving imbalanced class distributions across multiple datasets, incorporating the AUPRC provides additional insight into how well these models perform. The deep learning model also achieved the highest AUPRC (0.603), approximately four times the random-classifier baseline of 0.153. This suggests the model captures information relevant to iENE beyond what clinical variables and GTVn volume provide.

Because larger nodes are more likely to harbor ENE, we next assessed whether the model acts as a surrogate for nodal size. The model’s probabilistic output correlates only moderately with the volume of the patient’s largest locoregional lymph node (GTVn), with Pearson r = 0.335 and Spearman ρ = 0.587 (Figure 4.2). Although statistically significant, this association leaves most of the variation in model output unexplained by volume. Due to the discrete nature of ENE determinations, which are not continuous probabilities, a baseline correlation coefficient for these determinations cannot be calculated.

It is also important to consider the model’s performance on ambiguous samples, by observing outliers. The scatterplot shows expected false positives at larger GTVn volumes, and false negatives with smaller GTVn volumes, but the model also confidently rejects several cases with extremely large GTVn volumes, avoiding false positives that a volume-based model would not reject, demonstrating that the model integrates signal beyond size (Figure 4.2).

Performance of the model on the external validation set was stratified by the image modality used to clinically determine iENE (Figure 4.3, Table 4.3). The model performs best and most consistently on labels determined by CT or MRI, and moderately on PET-CT-determined labels. We note that the CT-labeled subset is small (n=21), and the AUROC and AUPRC estimates on this subset carry wide uncertainty; bootstrap confidence intervals are reported in Table 4.3 to quantify this. Modality-stratified performance should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive, with the aggregate evaluation across the full Maastro cohort (n=175) providing the most stable estimate of the model's external generalization.

4.4Chapter discussion

Reliable determination of iENE is crucial to effective HNSCC staging. Clinically overt ENE was incorporated into the 8th edition UICC/AJCC criteria for non-viral HNSCC but was explicitly not a staging factor for HPV-positive oropharyngeal carcinoma O'Sullivan 2017. It is not equivalent to iENE, a radiologic determination that captures earlier extranodal spread, such as perinodal fat infiltration or nodal coalescence, which would not meet clinical criteria Henson 2024. Unequivocal iENE is now incorporated into the 9th edition clinical N classification for HPV-associated oropharyngeal carcinoma, yet it remains difficult to determine consistently Huang 2025Brierley 2025Brierley 2026Sahin 2025. Pathologic ENE is unavailable in the majority of patients treated non-surgically and cannot inform the initial treatment decision, while radiologic iENE requires substantial expert training and is subject to meaningful interobserver variability Sahin 2025. In this study we developed a deep learning model that addresses this gap in three ways. First, it predicts iENE at the patient level and is trained directly on radiologist-determined iENE labels rather than pathologic ENE, so its output is interpretable on the same scale as the clinical determination it is intended to support. Second, it operates on radiation treatment-planning CT, imaging that is already routinely acquired for every patient, allowing direct deployment within the radiotherapy workflow without additional imaging or manual lymph node segmentation. Third, the full pipeline, trained weights, and software environment are released as open-source, reproducible resources. Together these choices distinguish our approach from prior AI-based ENE work and make the model clinically applicable as defined in section 1.7.

Our approach differs from prior AI-based ENE work in both the prediction target and the supervision signal, with implications for clinical interpretability and downstream utility. Kann and colleagues' original work, and the subsequent AI-ENE extension by Ye and colleagues, predict ENE at the level of individual lymph nodes using an auto-segmentation pipeline to enable end-to-end inference Kann 2018Kann 2020Ye 2026. Dayan and colleagues sit between these approaches, supervising on radiologist-determined iENE, but still classifying from a segmented lymph node, in a single-centre HPV-positive cohort without external validation Dayan 2026. Because these node-level models are trained with pENE as ground truth, they learn from a surgically treated population that is non-representative of the broader HNSCC cohort, much of which is treated non-surgically. Patient-level aggregation reported by Ye and colleagues yields at least one AI-ENE-positive node in 70.8% of cases, roughly four times the 15–20% iENE prevalence reported in radiologic series including our own, suggesting a model that is highly sensitive but not calibrated to a clinically actionable specificity Huang 2020Ye 2026. Without an iENE ground truth to anchor the patient-level aggregation, AI-ENE outputs cannot be directly compared to a radiologist's determination and are difficult to integrate into staging frameworks such as the 9th edition TNM, which is defined relative to iENE rather than pENE or AI-ENE Huang 2025O'Sullivan 2020.

In contrast, our model is anchored to radiologist-determined iENE and retained above-chance discrimination on both the internal held-out and external validation sets, albeit with a substantial drop externally. The Kaplan-Meier analyses indicate that its stratification of high- and low-risk patients is at least as prognostic as the radiologist's determination on which it was trained; at a higher-specificity threshold, the model's separation of distant-control outcomes is in fact more discriminative than the radiologist's binary determination, suggesting that it captures prognostic signal beyond simply recapitulating its training labels. This separation, however, primarily reflects specificity at the chosen operating point and does not directly characterize sensitivity, which must be assessed separately when considering deployment. Future work could examine whether combining the two paradigms, by using a sensitive node-level model to flag candidate locoregional nodes and a specific patient-level model to confirm clinically meaningful iENE, could outperform either approach alone, particularly for isolating high-risk subpopulations within HPV-positive disease.

Discrimination was lower in the Maastro cohort than in the RADCURE held-out set (AUROC 0.671 vs 0.913). AUPRC was nominally similar, but against a higher iENE prevalence (34.3% vs 15.3%), corresponding to a smaller gain over random chance (1.8x vs 3.9x) Saito 2015. Three key differences in the cohorts likely contribute to this change in performance (Table 4.1): the Maastro cohort includes no cN0 patients, who are readily classified as iENE-negative; the Maastro planning CTs were reconstructed at thicker slice thicknesses (3.0mm vs 2.0-2.5mm for RADCURE), affecting the nodal anatomy at different through-plane scales; the heterogeneity of labels incorporating diagnostic CT or MRI whereas the model only used planning (PET-)CT.

The threshold analyses across subpopulations highlight that a single model can support distinct clinical use cases by selecting an appropriate operating point. At a high-specificity threshold, the model functions as a staging and personalized prognostication aid, providing high-confidence iENE information to complement TNM staging. This is particularly relevant for HPV/p16-negative patients under the 8th edition UICC/AJCC criteria, where clinically overt ENE directly influences stage assignment and surgical candidacy, as well as for the 9th edition’s incorporation of iENE for HPV-associated disease Huang 2025Brierley 2025Brierley 2026. For non-viral disease, the UICC 9th edition retains clinical ENE as the N3b criterion but now accepts unequivocal imaging findings as evidence for it Brierley 2025Brierley 2026. Whether an automated iENE determination can support that assessment in HPV/p16-negative HNSCC is a question our openly released pipeline makes testable in larger, non-viral cohorts. At a higher-sensitivity threshold, the model shifts into a screening role, flagging patients who warrant radiologist review for iENE, which is not consistently part of standard clinical practice today. In this configuration, the model enriches the review queue rather than replacing radiologist judgment, allocating expert attention toward cases most likely to harbor iENE. The ECOG-ACRIN 3311 trial of transoral surgery and de-intensified radiotherapy (RT) for HPV/p16-positive oropharyngeal cancer (OPC) illustrates this need: it enrolled participants without matted nodes, yet 31% received subsequent chemoradiotherapy after being classified high-risk on the basis of positive margins, ENE, or a high number of metastatic lymph nodes, and accurate pre-enrollment determination of iENE might have spared some of these patients from tri-modality treatment Ferris 2022. This pattern is not confined to trial populations: in a real-world SEER-Medicare cohort of locoregionally advanced HNSCC, the majority of resected patients (56%) went on to receive post-operative radiotherapy with or without systemic therapy, escalation that is frequently triggered by pathologic ENE Zheng 2025. Prospective validation is essential to translate these threshold analyses into deployment-ready parameters, with operating points tuned to each institutional workflow and clinical question.

While the model's consistent performance increases the likelihood of clinical adoption, an essential contribution of this study lies in the reproducibility and accessibility of the solution. This work is developed entirely on publicly accessible datasets from The Cancer Imaging Archive Clark 2013, and the iENE labels and train/test split for RADCURE are released via its clinical spreadsheet on TCIA, allowing any researcher to reproduce this study end-to-end and iterate on our findings Welch 2024. The full training and inference codebases, along with trained model weights, are publicly available, with environment specifications managed by pixi for single-command installation in a carbon copy of the authors' environment. Both raw DICOM images from clinical PACS and pre-processed NIfTI images are supported as input, via the open-source Med-ImageTools curation package Kim 2025.

4.4.1Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. Most importantly, RADCURE iENE labels came from a single radiologist, so the model inherits that reader’s thresholds and the interobserver variability documented for iENE Hoebers 2022Sahin 2025; multi-reader labels would better define the achievable ceiling. The pipeline’s main computational bottleneck is nnU-Net-based larynx segmentation Isensee 2021, though a lighter detection model may suffice given that the iENE model’s 7-fold TTA already buffers against minor crop variation Ayhan 2018Shanmugam 2021; architectural gains from attention mechanisms or added parameters are also constrained by our limited training set and overfitting risk, motivating future federated learning across institutions. Training relied on implicit radiologist determinations rather than the explicit four-tier criteria recently proposed by the Head and Neck Cancer International Group Henson 2024, and training models to directly predict for these endpoints would improve interpretability. The model was trained on oropharyngeal cancer only, limiting generalizability to other HNSCC subsites where treatment escalation is also common, and is restricted to CT input despite MRI's superior soft-tissue contrast and its use as a ground-truth modality in our external validation cohort Huang 2020.

5.1Introduction

Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) of the jaw is a major late toxicity of head and neck radiotherapy. Reported prevalence varies widely, ranging from 4–8% in the modern IMRT era and with prophylactic dental care policies, to as high as 40% in historical series Watson 2024. Buglione and colleagues’ work on building multidisciplinary consensus concluded that poor dental health before RT and post-radiation extraction of teeth in the high dose irradiated region are considered the highest risk factors for ORN Buglione 2016. While there are no randomized controlled trials assessing safe extraction of teeth before or after RT, observational studies have generally demonstrated higher rates of ORN with post-RT extraction Støre 2000Thorn 2000, although some studies find no significant differences Epstein 1987Reuther 2003. The ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline now advises a two-week healing period between extraction and the start of radiotherapy, qualified by the requirement that it must not delay treatment where oncologic control would be compromised Peterson 2024.

Dose is already the operative variable in making pre-RT dental decisions, but it is only communicated to the dentist on the whole-jaw level. The Canadian Dental Oncology Network guidelines determine critical radiation thresholds for when a tooth at risk should be prophylactically extracted at 70 Gy in the maxilla and 60 Gy in the mandible Watson 2021. Whether a given tooth exceeds that threshold is precisely the quantity unavailable at the chairside. The ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline makes the communication requirement explicit, recommending that information on the planned treatment volume and the anticipated dose to the mandible and maxilla be provided to the dentist before radiotherapy begins Peterson 2024. In practice, this information is only reported at the level of the whole structure.

Van Dijk and colleagues developed RADMAP, which takes a semi-automated approach to determine per-tooth dose and demonstrate that the added spatial information of granular per-tooth dosimetry improves the predictive power for ORN compared to the crude mandible dosimetry. RADMAP segments the mandible and maxilla into 32 tooth-bearing regions using an angular ray-based algorithm and reports mean dose per region as a table and as a heat-mapped odontogram. Across 736 tooth locations in 23 patients, mean dose to the automatically segmented alveolar region correlated with manually contoured tooth roots at R²=0.98. Interobserver limits of agreement across six users were ±2.88 Gy in the mandibular alveolus and tooth-region dose separated ORN-affected from unaffected sites (p=0.0008), whereas whole-mandible mean dose did not (p=0.17) van Dijk 2025. However, manual delineation of the 32 tooth regions is a major obstacle to implementing orodental dose reporting in practice, requiring 35 to 120 minutes per patient depending on operator experience, and the RADMAP interactive tool reduces the burden rather than removing it completely van Dijk 2025. Dosarex addresses the automation gap by augmenting routine panoramic radiographs with the annotation of the per-tooth planning dose, produced end-to-end from the planning DICOM study without human intervention. As with the iENE model, the aim is clinical applicability, here for tooth extraction.

5.2Methods and preliminary results

From planning CT to a dose on every tooth

Dosarex runs these steps unattended for each patient. Select a step to read what it does and open its figure.

Dataset. We leveraged an institutional subset of the RADCURE cohort (n = 885; Table 2.1) for which a routine panorex and the planning RTDOSE were both retrievable from our imaging PACS via MIRA; dose and panorex data are not part of the public RADCURE release. The CT, RTSTRUCT and RTDOSE files were processed using Med-ImageTools Kim 2025, which were used to develop the curved planar reformation module. The projected CTs were used to train the nnU-Net model, and SemiT-SAM, fine-tuned on a public dataset, was applied to the panorex images.

Curved planar reformation (CPR) is implemented headlessly in 3DSlicer, adapted from the PerkLab’s SlicerSandbox module, CurvedPlanarReformat function Pieper 2004. The medial curve of the mandible mask is calculated, then a straightening transform is computed along the curve. The calculated straightening transform is applied to the CT and dose volumes, then the maximum intensity is projected depthwise, producing a synthetic panorex and its corresponding dose projection.

Tooth landmarks in each modality are obtained through a two-stage process. Each tooth is first segmented independently in the panorex and in the projected CT, after which the centroid of each segmented tooth is computed and used as its corresponding landmark.

Tooth segmentation on panoramic radiographs was performed using SemiT-SAM Hao 2025. We initialized the network with its publicly released pretrained checkpoint and fine-tuned on STS24-2Dxray Wang 2026: with 30 labeled and 2,350 unlabeled panoramic radiographs, following the training protocol described by Hao and colleagues. The fine-tuned model was used to obtain per-tooth instance segmentation masks on panorex (Figure 5.2).

Tooth segmentation on the projected CT images was performed using nnU-Net, trained on a manually annotated dataset of 150 cases. Each case was labeled with a three-class map distinguishing background, tooth interior, and tooth boundary, with the boundary class introduced to help the network separate closely spaced or touching teeth that would otherwise be difficult to distinguish. The trained network was applied to our projected CT images to obtain tooth segmentation masks for each case.

Since this semantic output does not directly separate individual tooth instances, a post-processing step was applied to the segmented tooth regions to recover per-tooth instances, correcting for occasional cases where two or more adjacent teeth were merged into a single connected region, whether horizontally or vertically.

Correspondence between teeth landmarks of the projected CT and panorex was established using the Hungarian algorithm (Figure 5.4) Kuhn 1955. It uses the centroids of the tooth instance segmentations extracted for each modality and the point sets are normalized by centering and scaling to a common coordinate frame, since the two modalities differ substantially in image scale and resolution.

Using the resulting matched landmark pairs, the registration function was estimated between the CT and panoramic coordinate spaces, with the transformation consisting of rotation, uniform scaling, and translation. Using least-median-of-squares minimization for the estimation allowed the transformation to remain stable even if the Hungarian matching produces a small number of erroneous correspondences. The estimated transformation was then applied to warp the projected CT into the panoramic coordinate space. We evaluated registration accuracy by calculating the mean Euclidean distance between the projected CT and panorex landmarks.

The 3D treatment planning dose was warped into the panoramic coordinate space using CPR and the similarity transformation estimated during registration, allowing the same dose distribution to be expressed in panoramic image coordinates. For each detected tooth in the panorex, the mean warped dose was computed within a correspondingly dilated tooth mask, scaled to account for the difference in image resolution between the two modalities (Figure 5.6). The same process was applied to the projected CT, and the dose values for each matched tooth pair were compared, yielding a per-tooth measure of dose consistency across modalities. The results of the Dosarex pipeline are visualized for the clinician in a report with the tooth segmentations overlaid on its corresponding image, with each tooth colored according to its mean computed dose (Figure 5.7).

5.3Chapter discussion / Next steps

Dosarex is reproducible and deployable without manual intervention. However, for clinical adoption, it must be externally validated to show it can reliably inform extraction decisions. Using per-tooth dose with clinical features to predict per-tooth ORN risk can validate the accuracy of the pipeline’s outputs and its clinical implications. Existing literature on per-tooth dose-ORN analysis rests on 23 patients and 21 affected tooth regions van Dijk 2025, but a fully automated pipeline like Dosarex can be run retrospectively across an institutional cohort and produce per-tooth dosimetry. Using the ClinRad severity classification Watson 2024, which grades the vertical extent of necrosis and the presence of exposed bone or fistula, we can train for graded and granular clinical outcomes rather than gross ORN, providing clinicians with actionable insights to make specific and intentional decisions.

Furthermore, if per-tooth dose is available automatically at planning time, it becomes an input to plan optimization rather than only a reporting output. The mandibular NTCP model constrains 30% of the mandible to 35 Gy or 42 Gy depending on pre-radiotherapy extraction status van Dijk 2021, and the ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline advises reducing the mean dose to the jaw and the volume of bone above 50 Gy wherever possible Peterson 2024. Per-tooth dose estimation would also allow radiation oncologists to determine whether dose can be traded between tooth regions according to which teeth the dentist intends to retain.

How to adopt machine learning models into the clinic

The distance between a raw, clinical dataset and a complex clinical decision is usually described as a modelling problem. This thesis measured that distance differently.

6.1Summary of contributions

This thesis contributed, in order:

  • RADCURE: The largest public head and neck CT collection (n=3,346)

  • Med-ImageTools: An open-source Python package for medical imaging dataset processing validated at scale

  • Three evaluation studies investigating what imaging models actually learn:

    • RADCURE-challenge: institution-wide challenge to predict survival and personalized prognosis

    • READII-2-ROQC: a nine-condition negative-control framework

    • SCARF: a quantitative evaluation of segmentation models and blinded clinical acceptability benchmark

  • Patient-level iENE model, externally validated, with comprehensive model evaluations, including varying clinical operating thresholds

  • Dosarex, a fully automated pipeline that reports radiation dose per tooth on routine panoramic radiographs, without any manual contouring or operator intervention.

  • Miscellaneous methods works include: Deep-CR-MTLR, FastCPH, FAIR-HNC, and jarvAIs.

6.2Reproducibility as a scientific result

Reproducibility in this thesis is not a stated virtue but an evidenced property, and the evidence is largely not self-reported. Med-ImageTools is the processing engine for all of the projects built on the RADCURE dataset: SCARF's CONSORT-AI accounting credits Med-ImageTools data curation with 240 of 450 developer-hours saved; READII-2-ROQC builds its provenance layer on the same tooling. And the iENE model ships as a fully reproducible study, from public training data, released labels and split, weights, and locked environment—contributions equal in weight to its AUROC, allowing an external group to scrutinize the held-out result and test on their own datasets without our involvement.

jarvAIs is an open-source Python package for reproducible machine learning research on oncology data. It packages every decision along the development process into reproducible, configurable settings, including training data auditing, model hyperparameter selection, feature reduction and selection choices, validation splits, bias detection, model interpretability modules, and post-hoc fairness audits. We compared jarvAIs to AIM2REDUCE-ED, a hand-built model for 30-day emergency department visits during systemic therapy at Princess Margaret, currently in silent deployment Grant 2023Kabir 2024. The automated jarvAIs pipeline matched it on the held-out set (AUROC 0.70 vs 0.69, AUPRC 0.23 vs 0.20), trailed it modestly in the prospective cohort (0.67 vs 0.69, AUPRC 0.11 vs 0.16), and identified the treatment-regimen subgroups a manual bias analysis had flagged, in under thirty minutes of development time Siraj 2025. Reproducibility does not have to be expensive if the infrastructure and tooling are developed intentionally and used as the default path, paving a translational highway for machine learning models into the clinic.

6.3Evaluating cancer imaging models

The RADCURE challenge (Ch. 3.2) measured the tumour volume confounder in the wild. Engineered radiomics predictions correlated with volume at ρ=0.79 to 0.85, and the winning model used EMR features plus volume explicitly. The same pattern appeared in our multi-institutional federated study, where a Cox model on clinical variables (n=1,492) reached a C-index of 0.62 to 0.73 across nodes, a radiomics-only model (n=1,321) reached 0.61 to 0.68, and the mixed model reached 0.60 to 0.72, with no improvement over the clinical model alone Gouthamchand 2024. READII-2-ROQC (Ch. 3.3) turned the observation into a controlled test. The Aerts signature survived nine negative controls unchanged and never separated from the volume-only baseline, and the foundation model's features were shown to derive from the background rather than the tumour. SCARF (Ch. 3.4) demonstrated that geometric rankings and clinical acceptability rankings diverge.

The common lesson is that AUROC, DSC and C-index are proxies of the underlying metric we’re actually interested in. Each is chosen because it is tractable, not because it is the quantity a clinician cares about, and we believe a proxy's job is to predict how well a model serves a specific decision. SCARF demonstrates that a proxy can do that job measurably poorly at the extremes, and the RADCURE-challenge and READII results show that a strong number can describe tumour size or surrounding context rather than the tumour itself. None of this means imaging is uninformative. Model 9 from the RADCURE-challenge reached AUROC 0.766 at ρ=0.22, and READII identified a class of features that responds to structured perturbation while remaining independent of volume. Imaging signal is learnable, but it must be demonstrated by construction. Volume correlation analysis, negative controls, ablation evidence, and superiority over a volume baseline are what can separate a model that has learned valuable features beyond tumour volume, and we believe they should be as routine as cross-validation.

6.4Models must be built around a decision

The iENE model in Chapter 4 was designed against the findings above rather than evaluated against them after the fact. Its correlation with the largest nodal volume was characterized directly (Pearson r = 0.335, Spearman ρ = 0.587), it confidently rejected several cases with very large GTVn, and it scored above the volume baseline on both AUROC and AUPRC. We used ACS convolutions on the full neck volume rather than on segmented nodes, removing the dependency on a lymph node contour—an auto-segmentation step that, as SCARF showed even for well-defined OARs, cannot be assumed clinically acceptable on the strength of geometric metrics. What the model could not remove is the variability in the radiologist labels it was trained on, but we instead leveraged the clinician’s labels to compare against the model’s predictions to characterize its prognostic performance. We evaluated the effect of varying decision thresholds, acknowledging that one model serves two decisions with different costs of error, and treated τ as a clinical dial rather than a nuisance parameter.

Dosarex is the clearest case in this thesis of a design driven by the decision rather than by the data. The decision is whether to extract a specific tooth before radiotherapy, and its parameters are set externally. The Canadian Dental Oncology Network defines prophylactic extraction thresholds of 70 Gy in the maxilla and 60 Gy in the mandible Watson 2021, and the ISOO-MASCC-ASCO guideline asks that the anticipated dose to the jaw be communicated to the dentist before treatment begins Peterson 2024. The unit of that decision is the individual tooth, but dose is conventionally reported for the whole structure. Van Dijk and colleagues showed that the whole mandible mean dose could not reliably predict ORN, whereas per-tooth region dose could van Dijk 2025. Dosarex reports the radiotherapy dose estimate on the panoramic radiograph a dentist already reads, at the level of the individual tooth on which the extraction decision can be made. The pipeline is fully end-to-end automated, from the planning DICOM study to the routinely acquired panorex, because 35-120 minutes of manual delineation per patient is too significant a cost for integration into a clinical workflow van Dijk 2025.

6.5Future directions

Our studies can continue into several different directions:

Federated learning addresses both the training-scale limit and the single-institution limit without relocating sensitive patient data. Gouthamchand and colleagues federated five head and neck cohorts that shared no schema at their point of origin, comprising HN1, HNSCC, HEAD-NECK, a private MAASTRO dataset, and the Princess Margaret OPC-Radiomics collection, which our group prepared and hosted as the Canadian node. Data content was decoupled from schema into RDF, annotated with the Radiation Oncology Ontology and the NCI Thesaurus as a metadata layer, and read back through a single semantic query. Federated Cox models were then trained and validated across the network by leave-one-dataset-out internal-external validation under the Personal Health Train paradigm, with no patient-level data leaving any station Gouthamchand 2024. The infrastructure to federate both conventional and deep learning models already exists, and international collaborations have been successfully demonstrated Choudhury 2025. Chapter 4 showed that architectural gains from attention or added capacity are constrained by a training set of 752 patients, and that a single external cohort with a different case mix reduced AUROC from 0.913 to 0.671. A federated iENE study across the institutions that already contribute to these networks is the most direct way to test whether the model's performance is limited by data or by the problem. Med-ImageTools was designed with this use in mind, since each participating node can run the same processing pipelines without requiring any manual curation and produce identically structured ML-ready data.

Better labels, better decisions. iENE is a radiologist determination with documented interobserver variability Hoebers 2022Sahin 2025. Every model trained on it inherits that variability as a ceiling, and the single-reader labels used in our study are the greatest limitation Kim 2026. Hoebers and colleagues showed that concordance is trainable, with inter-institutional kappa rising from 0.40 to 0.65 once operating definitions were consolidated Hoebers 2022. The Head and Neck Cancer International Group consensus criteria published in 2024 replaced an informal reading of iENE with a four-tier classification and an explicit rule for equivocal findings, and all seventeen surveyed experts said they would adopt synoptic reporting in routine practice Henson 2024. This means that grade-level, multi-reader labels can be generated prospectively as part of routine reporting rather than as a separate research exercise. A model trained on HNCIG synoptic reports would produce outputs that map directly onto the 9th edition staging variable, distinguishing perinodal fat extension from conglomerate nodes from invasion of adjacent structures and would be interpretable against criteria that carry known diagnostic confidence. This is the change most likely to move the iENE model from a research result to a clinical instrument, and it depends more on reporting practice than on modelling.

Beyond oropharynx. The iENE model was trained on a cohort only with oropharyngeal cancer patients. Treatment escalation driven by ENE is also common in oral cavity and laryngeal disease, and the 9th edition now uses unequivocal iENE as a criterion for cN3 in non-viral head and neck cancer Brierley 2026, so the same prediction target applies across subsites. Extending our study to other subsites will test the model’s generalizability and reveal whether to expand the training set to non-OPC cohorts with iENE labels.

Beyond CT. The iENE model is restricted to CT despite the superior soft-tissue contrast of MRI, and the Maastro validation showed that labels made on MRI were predicted nearly as well as labels made on CT. This suggests the planning CT carries more of the relevant signal than is usually assumed, but a direct comparison with MRI input, or a multimodal model where both are available, has not yet been done in existing literature, according to our best knowledge.

Deployment is an art. Every model in this thesis was built to be run by someone else. The remaining step is to make running them routine rather than possible. SCARF is already embedded in MIRA, the institutional platform that extracted RADCURE, and the jarvAIs API service was built to serve models into the same infrastructure. Serving the iENE model and the Dosarex pipeline through MIRA, with Med-ImageTools handling DICOM input and containerizing our software with fully reproducible environments, would close the translational loop. This integration would blaze the trail for the next area of research of implementation science, investigating how AI systems augment and impact clinical decision making.

6.6Concluding remarks

The distance between a raw, clinical dataset and a complex clinical decision is usually described as a modelling problem. This thesis measured that distance differently. First, curation is a scientific step and should be treated as one. The choices made while converting DICOM into model inputs shape every downstream result, and Med-ImageTools exists so that those choices are written down, versioned, and repeatable. Second, a performance metric is a claim about a model that must itself be tested. The volume confounder, the negative-control results, and the gap between geometric accuracy and clinical acceptability all show that a strong number can describe something other than what it appears to describe. Third, a model earns clinical relevance by being aimed at a decision. Our iENE model’s output lives on the scale a radiologist uses and a staging system now requires, and Dosarex reports dose at the level a dentist acts on. None of this replaces the clinician—the iENE model enriches a review queue, Dosarex informs a chairside decision, and both leave the critical judgment where it belongs. What our studies offer is a way of building these tools so that the judgment they support is better informed, and the evidence behind them can be checked by anyone who wants to. An imaging AI model is trustworthy when its data pipeline is transparent and reproducible, its signal is thoroughly characterized, and its output is aimed at a real clinical decision to assist responsibly.
